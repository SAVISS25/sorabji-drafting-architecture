\## Section 1 — Macro Architecture Manual (Sorabji structural skeleton)

\### A. Overall blueprint (chapter/paper-level)

A Sorabji-style piece in this excerpt is built as a \*\*three-stage chain\*\* with explicit signposting:

1\. \*\*Introduction that reframes a live policy/technical question as a rule-of-law problem\*\* (normative stakes established immediately).

2\. \*\*Diagnosis of pressures/challenges\*\* (often in paired form: \*funding\* + \*technology\*; \*private\* + \*public\*; \*individual\* + \*systemic\*).

3\. \*\*Analytical pivot\*\*: concede contingency in narrow case-types, then \*\*reassert necessity\*\* at the constitutional/public-function level.

4\. \*\*Conclusion that returns to reform design obligations\*\* (not mere preferences), typically restating with elevation and warning.

In this chapter, the section sequence is explicit:

\- \*\*12.1 Introduction\*\* — rhetorical hook + thesis map.

\- \*\*12.2 Challenges\*\* — two challenges, each unfolded with sub-strands and consequences.

\- \*\*12.3 Contingency\*\* — explores the case for lawyerless justice; empirical support; conditional acceptance.

\- \*\*12.4 Necessity\*\* — the constitutional/public interest turn (open justice, right to advice, de-skilling).

\- \*\*12.5 Conclusion\*\* — balanced embrace of tech + insistence on defined essential role for lawyers.

\*\*Replication instruction:\*\* design your outline so that \*the most powerful normative claim\* appears \*\*after\*\* you have conceded the strongest practical case against it.

\---

\### B. The precise rhetorical move in the first paragraph

He opens with a \*\*high-recognition cultural quotation\*\* used as a \*\*diagnostic instrument\*\*, then immediately flips it from cliché into constitutional warning.

Verifiable examples:

\- Starts with quotation + immediate framing:

“\*The first thing we do, let’s kill all the lawyers.\*” → “\*On one level… necessary evil. At another level… hopes of authoritarians… an end to lawyers would mean an end to law.\*”

\- Immediate constitutional anchoring via aphorism:

“\*‘Whereever law ends, tyranny begins’.\*”

\*\*Replication instruction (opening paragraph recipe):\*\*

1\. Begin with a \*\*canonical line\*\* (literary/judicial/political) that “everyone knows.”

2\. Treat it as \*\*two-level rhetoric\*\* (“On one level… At another level…”).

3\. Convert level-two into a \*\*rule-of-law proposition\*\* (tyranny / accountability / legitimacy).

4\. Signal that the popular line is \*\*misleading unless properly understood\*\*.

\---

\### C. Relationship between introduction and conclusion (restate vs elevate)

He \*\*maps the argument\*\* in the introduction and \*\*returns to it\*\* in the conclusion with:

\- \*\*Qualified concession\*\* (“technology will… reduce necessity in many cases”)

\- \*\*Elevated restatement\*\* (lawyers as essential to open justice, participation, accountability)

\- \*\*Forward-looking warning\*\* (designing-out lawyers designs-out constitutional features)

Examples:

\- Intro forecasts a three-part structure: “\*first… Secondly… Finally…\*”

\- Conclusion mirrors but elevates: “\*Such developments should, however, take account of the need to ensure the involvement in lawyers… for principled reasons… broader public interest…\*”

\*\*Replication instruction:\*\* in your conclusion, do not merely restate. Do:

\- \*\*Concede the instrumental benefits\*\* of the opposing reform.

\- \*\*Reassert the constitutional function\*\* your thesis protects.

\- \*\*Translate thesis into design constraints\*\* (“reform needs to…”).

\---

\### D. Section functions in the logical chain

Each section performs a distinct job:

\- \*\*Introduction:\*\* sets stakes + disarms simplistic narrative + states roadmap.

\- \*\*Challenges/Context:\*\* creates inevitability pressure (“two challenges… funding… technological”).

\- \*\*Contingency section:\*\* grants conditional validity to reformers; uses empirical literature to support limited claims; narrows where lawyers are “non-essential.”

\- \*\*Necessity section:\*\* the \*turn\*—shifts from outcomes in individual cases to \*\*public functions\*\* (open justice; accountability; constitutional right; profession capacity).

\- \*\*Conclusion:\*\* reconciles innovation with constitutional constraint; ends with obligation and warning.

\*\*Replication instruction:\*\* label at least one mid-piece section explicitly as a \*contingency\* or \*limits\* discussion to show analytical fairness before the normative turn.

\---

\### E. Counter-argument placement

He places the \*\*strongest counter-case\*\* (lawyers add delay/cost without improving correctness) \*\*before\*\* his positive constitutional case, and he supports it with \*\*empirical authority\*\* rather than straw-manning.

Examples:

\- “\*Is the absence of lawyers… necessarily a negative development?\*”

\- Cites studies via Barton & Bibas and derives the strongest anti-lawyer conclusion: “\*Adding lawyers had perverse effects…\*”

\*\*Replication instruction:\*\* give the counter-argument its best evidence \*\*in its own section\*\*, then pivot by changing the evaluation metric (from \*individual outcome efficiency\* to \*systemic public functions\*).

\---

\### F. Limitations acknowledgement placement

Limitations and contingencies are \*\*embedded\*\* rather than quarantined:

\- “\*in some classes of litigation… may not be an essential feature… for some purposes\*”

\- “\*In the short term… this may not have a negative impact… Overtime though…\*”

\*\*Replication instruction:\*\* use \*\*temporal limitation\*\* (“short term/over time”) and \*\*scope limitation\*\* (“in some classes / for some purposes”) to keep claims precise.

\---

\### G. Transition signalling — exact transition language he uses

He uses \*\*sequencing adverbs\*\*, \*\*explicit roadmaps\*\*, and \*\*“turn” phrases\*\*.

Common transition language in the excerpt (use these patterns):

\- Roadmap: “\*In this chapter, first… Secondly… Finally…\*”

\- Stage shift: “\*Having outlined… it goes on to examine…\*”

\- Pivot: “\*The question then becomes…\*”

\- Scope move: “\*Is the absence… necessarily…?\*”

\- Corrective: “\*On the contrary, however,\* …”

\- Summative inference: “\*The inevitable consequence of this is that…\*”

\- Controlled concession: “\*It might be said…\* / \*There may be… however…\*”

\- Conclusion cue: “\*Where might this leave…?\*”

\*\*Replication instruction:\*\* every section opening should contain (i) an anaphoric reference backward (“Given this…”, “In addition…”), and (ii) a prospective statement (“The question then becomes…”).

1\. \*\*Binary framing / dual-level distinction\*\*

\- “\*On one level… At another level…\*”

2\. \*\*Governing question\*\*

\- “\*Is the absence of lawyers… necessarily a negative development?\*”

3\. \*\*Definitional/structural proposition\*\*

\*\*Replication instruction:\*\* start paragraphs with \*a governance sentence\* that tells the reader what the paragraph is \*doing\* (defining, questioning, distinguishing, or consequence-mapping).

\---

\### C. Ratio of claim : evidence : application (typical internal mix)

A common Sorabji paragraph (especially in analytic sections) runs approximately:

\- \*\*40–50% claim sentences\*\* (asserting structure/consequence)

\- \*\*30–40% evidence sentences\*\* (authority, reports, empirical studies, case law)

\- \*\*10–20% application sentences\*\* (what it implies for system design / access / legitimacy)

You see this clearly where he:

\- states a proposition (“removal of legal aid… consequence”),

\- supports with figures and official sources,

\- then derives system effects (“inevitable consequence… fundamentals… negatively affected”).

\### D. Five common paragraph-closing moves (how he ends analytical paragraphs)

1\. \*\*“Inevitable consequence” closure\*\* (deterministic causal seal)

Example: “\*The inevitable consequence of this is that…\*”

2\. \*\*“Question then becomes” forward hook\*\* (sets next section)

Example: “\*The question then becomes what effect that might have…\*”

3\. \*\*Scope-limiting closure\*\* (precision hedge)

Example: “\*…may not be essential… for some purposes… overall…\*”

4\. \*\*Normative warning closure\*\* (rule-of-law risk)

Example: “\*…would design-out the very features… committed to… the rule of law.\*”

5\. \*\*Reclassification closure\*\* (what this \*really\* is)

Example pattern: “\*A civil justice system does not, however, simply exist to… It has a wider public purpose…\*”

\*\*Replication instruction:\*\* end paragraphs either by (a) locking causation, or (b) opening the next analytic necessity (“The question then becomes…”), but avoid “soft fades.”

\### E. Concession pattern (“It might be said… but…”) placement

Yes—he uses it, typically \*\*late-middle\*\* of the paragraph, after stating the initial objection and before the rebuttal.

Example:

\- “\*It might be said…\*” → immediately followed by “\*It could, quite properly, be said…\*” → then “\*Here the final difficulty… arises…\*”

\*\*Replication instruction:\*\* place the concession \*\*after\*\* you have already framed the concern (so it reads as fair-minded), and then pivot with “however” into the deeper structural critique.

\### Move 3) The historical trajectory move (long revolution / steps / accretive change)

\*\*How:\*\*

Examples:

\- “three steps in the long revolution…”

\- “crossing of the Rubicon…”

\- “There have so far been \*\*\[number\]\*\* steps in \*\*\[trajectory\]\*\*. The next step will have to grapple with \*\*\[core rationale\]\*\*… It is important to assay the steps that led up to it…”

\### Move 4) The “this is not X, it is Y” reclassification move (and the sibling “not all questions…” scope move)

\- “Not all these questions are examined here…”

Replication template:

\- “Not all \*\*\[adjacent questions\]\*\* are examined here. This chapter/article instead focuses on \*\*\[narrow target\]\*\*: in particular, \*\*\[sub-questions\]\*\*.”

\---

\### Move 5) Reform-as-obligation move (policy recommendations as principled necessities)

\*\*How:\*\*

He makes reform conditional on fidelity to a governing principle (overriding objective; Article 6; proper administration).

Examples:

\- “Any future steps will… have to grapple… A fuller understanding… is a precondition…”

\- Conclusion: reform “must therefore be consistent with the overriding objective…”

Replication template:

\- “If \*\*\[system\]\*\* is to satisfy \*\*\[principle\]\*\*, then reform must \*\*\[design obligation\]\*\*. A \*\*\[fuller understanding/clear guidance/regulation\]\*\* is a precondition for \*\*\[future development\]\*\*.”

\---

\### Move 6) Closing sentence move (legacy + constraint + future orientation)

\*\*How:\*\*

End with a future-facing constraint statement tying reform’s next step to the animating spirit/principle.

Examples:

\- “Whatever the next step… we are sure that it will and must be one that is consistent with the spirit of Woolf’s reforms…”

\- Chapter 12: future with lawyers designed-out undermines rule-of-law commitments.

Replication template:

\- “Whatever the next step in \*\*\[domain\]\*\* may be, it will and must be consistent with \*\*\[governing principle\]\*\*; otherwise \*\*\[systemic cost\]\*\* will be paid in \*\*\[access/legitimacy/accountability\]\*\*.”

\## Section 7 — One-page Sorabji Style Guide (submission checklist)

\*\*Macro\*\*

1\. Structure as: \*\*stakes + roadmap → pressures/backdrop → concession/contingency → principled necessity → design-obligation conclusion\*\*.

6\. Close with: a \*\*question hook\*\*, a \*\*triad summary\*\*, or a \*\*constraint (“must”)\*\*.

7\. Prefer \*\*long sentences for layered qualification\*\*, but keep the spine visible with “however / therefore / in other words”.
