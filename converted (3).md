# Module 9: HD Drafting Protocol — Sorabji Method  
Version 1.0 • Confidence level: verified signatures + strong tendencies • Date: 2026-03-17

## Foundational Principles of the Sorabji Method (derived by majority count across 10 analyses)
1. **Counter-case first, constitutional pivot** (confirmed signature ≥7/10): present the strongest opposing case with its best evidence before pivoting to constitutional/public-function necessity as the governing metric.  
2. **Design obligation framing** (confirmed signature ≥7/10): reforms are cast as mandatory design constraints tied to an overriding principle (rule of law, access to justice, open justice), not preferences.  
3. **Enumerated, scaffolded progression** (strong tendency 6/10+): arguments advance through numbered/triadic sequences with explicit transitions (“first… secondly… finally… therefore”), maintaining a visible spine for long sentences and sections.

Conflict resolution rule: when analyses diverged, patterns were classified by frequency—confirmed signature (≥7/10), strong tendency (4–6/10), occasional (<4/10). Occasional items are relegated to the supplementary appendix.

## Component 1 — The Paper Architecture Blueprint  
*Purpose: provide an executable section-by-section blueprint with explicit moves, proportions, and Sorabji-specific deviations.*

1. **Opening paragraph (8–10% words) — diagnostic hook → constitutional frame**  
   - Opening move: start with a high-recognition quotation/aphorism treated as two-level rhetoric (“On one level… At another level…”).  
   - Function: reframe a live policy/technical issue as a rule-of-law problem.  
   - Closing move: state roadmap with numbered steps and foreshadow the constitutional metric (“first… secondly… finally… judged by open justice/accountability”).  
   - Deviation: uses cultural quotation as diagnostic instrument (purpose: immediate authority + reclassification from cliché to constitutional warning).  
2. **Literature gap identification (8–10%) — scope discipline**  
   - Opening move: declare what is “beyond scope/outwith ambit,” then name the narrow gap.  
   - Function: signal discipline and prevent straw targets.  
   - Closing move: pose a forward question (“The question that arises at this point in time is…”).  
3. **Thesis statement placement (2–3%) — explicit, early, necessity-framed**  
   - Placement: end of the opening section or start of gap section.  
   - Form: “\[\[Principled Necessity\]\] must govern \[\[Reform Domain\]\] to satisfy \[\[Constitutional Principle\]\].”  
   - Closing move: tie thesis to design obligation (“must be consistent with the overriding objective”).  
4. **Context and pressures (12–15%) — paired drivers**  
   - Opening move: “This arose as a consequence of \[\[Driver_1\]\] and \[\[Driver_2\]\].”  
   - Function: create inevitability pressure.  
   - Closing move: enumerate consequences in a triad.  
5. **Positive case development (20–25%) — doctrinal/empirical support**  
   - Opening move: definition anchored to authority or timeline clause.  
   - Function: build doctrinal foundation and practical stakes.  
   - Closing move: causal seal (“The consequence of that shift has been…”).  
6. **Counter-argument section (12–15%) — strongest opposing case before pivot**  
   - Opening move: binary question (“Is the absence of lawyers necessarily a negative development?”).  
   - Function: present best empirical/theoretical case against thesis.  
   - Evidence: cite empirical studies; avoid strawman.  
   - Closing move: shift evaluation metric (“The question is really one of \[\[Constitutional Metric\]\], not \[\[Efficiency Metric\]\].”).  
7. **Contingency/limitations acknowledgement (8–10%) — embedded concessions**  
   - Opening move: “It might however be said that \[\[Objection\]\]…”  
   - Function: acknowledge scope limits and conditionality.  
   - Closing move: regulation/design answer + evidence gap (“Proper regulation can deal with such problems; it is not clear that…”).  
8. **Normative turn: constitutional necessity (15–18%) — elevation move**  
   - Opening move: “At another level, \[\[System\]\] must \[\[Constitutional Function\]\].”  
   - Function: reclassify debate from case-level outcomes to public-function obligations.  
   - Closing move: obligation clause with “must/essential/paramount.”  
9. **Reform derivation (10–12%) — obligation-based design**  
   - Opening move: “If \[\[System\]\] is to satisfy \[\[Principle\]\], reform must \[\[Design Constraint\]\].”  
   - Function: derive design rules as necessities.  
   - Closing move: precondition statement (“A fuller understanding/clear guidance is a precondition for \[\[Next Step\]\].”).  
10. **Closing sentence (2–3%) — future-facing constraint**  
    - Opening move: recap conditional future (“Whatever the next step in \[\[Domain\]\] may be…”).  
    - Function: crystallise principle + warning.  
    - Closing move: constraint + cost (“…it will and must be consistent with \[\[Principle\]\]; otherwise \[\[Systemic Cost\]\] will follow.”).  
    - Deviation: ends with design constraint rather than summary; purpose is to leave reader with non-negotiable rule.

## Component 2 — The Paragraph Template  
*Purpose: supply fillable paragraph templates for Sorabji’s confirmed architecture.*

### Positive analytical paragraph (confirmed signature)
- **Opening move:** counter-intuitive claim.  
- **Template with named slots:**  
  1. `[Opening_CounterIntuitive_Claim]` (“It is tempting to assume \[\[Assumption\]\]; yet \[\[Contrary Claim\]\].”)  
  2. `[Claim_Core]` (“\[\[Proposition\]\] because \[\[Reason_1\]\] and \[\[Reason_2\]\].”)  
  3. `[Evidence_Authority]` (“As \[\[Authority\]\] shows, \[\[Quoted/Paraphrased Point\]\].”)  
  4. `[Application]` (“This means that \[\[Practical/Doctrinal Consequence\]\].”)  
  5. `[Concession_Embedded]` (“It might be said that \[\[Objection\]\]; however, \[\[Neutralisation\]\].”)  
  6. `[Closing_Move]` (“The consequence of that shift has been \[\[Outcome\]\].”)  

### Counter-argument paragraph (confirmed signature)
- **Opening move:** binary question.  
- **Template:**  
  1. `[Opening_Binary_Question]` (“Is \[\[Target\]\] necessarily \[\[Negative/Positive\]\]?”)  
  2. `[Counter_Claim]` (“\[\[Opposing Proposition\]\] holds where \[\[Conditions\]\].”)  
  3. `[Evidence_Empirical]` (“\[\[Study/Case\]\] found \[\[Result\]\].”)  
  4. `[Application_Shift]` (“In such contexts, \[\[Outcome\]\] follows.”)  
  5. `[Concession_To_Main]` (“This does not mean \[\[Extreme\]\]; it shows \[\[Limited Point\]\].”)  
  6. `[Closing_Pivot]` (“The question is really one of \[\[Constitutional/Public Metric\]\], not \[\[Efficiency Metric\]\].”)  

### Reform-derivation paragraph (strong tendency)
- **Opening move:** necessity claim tied to principle.  
- **Template:**  
  1. `[Opening_Necessity]` (“If \[\[System\]\] is to satisfy \[\[Principle\]\], reform must \[\[Design Obligation\]\].”)  
  2. `[Claim_Design]` (“That requires \[\[Design Element 1\]\] and \[\[Design Element 2\]\].”)  
  3. `[Evidence_Normative/Authority]` (“Article \[\[X\]\]/\[\[Case\]\]/\[\[Report\]\] confirms \[\[Requirement\]\].”)  
  4. `[Application_FutureStep]` (“Without \[\[Design Element\]\], \[\[Risk\]\] follows.”)  
  5. `[Concession_Modality]` (“While modalities may vary, the constraint is non-negotiable.”)  
  6. `[Closing_Precondition]` (“A fuller understanding/clear guidance is a precondition for \[\[Next Step\]\].”)

## Component 3 — The Sentence Pattern Library  
*Purpose: provide ready-to-use sentence templates grouped by rhetorical function; use only when the listed conditions match.*

### Reclassification patterns (≥5; most important)
1. **Not X but Y reframing (confirmed signature)**  
   - Template: “\[\[Target\]\] is not \[\[Common Frame\]\]; it is \[\[Reframed Category\]\].”  
   - Example: “Lawyerlessness is not simply a cost problem; it is a constitutional design problem” (Justice Without Lawyers).  
   - Function: shift evaluative metric. Use when moving debate from efficiency to principle; do not use for trivial distinctions.  
2. **Question re-set pattern**  
   - Template: “The question is really one of \[\[Metric A\]\], not \[\[Metric B\]\].”  
   - Example: “The question is really one of design and superiority” (Litigation Funding chapter).  
   - Use when redirecting evaluation; avoid when both metrics align.  
3. **From exception to rule**  
   - Template: “What appears to be \[\[Exception\]\] is in fact \[\[Rule/Structural Feature\]\].”  
   - Example: “Austerity’s case backlog is not an aberration; it is a structural consequence of funding design” (Austerity article).  
   - Use when evidencing systemic pattern; avoid if data are singular.  
4. **Dual-level meaning**  
   - Template: “On one level \[\[Meaning1\]\]; at another level \[\[Meaning2\]\].”  
   - Example: Shakespeare quotation flipped to constitutional warning (Justice Without Lawyers).  
   - Use for opening diagnostics; avoid mid-paragraph repetition.  
5. **Reclassification via function**  
   - Template: “\[\[Process/Tool\]\] is not merely \[\[Mechanism\]\]; it functions as \[\[Public/Constitutional Role\]\].”  
   - Example: “Open justice is not simply transparency; it is a participation guarantee” (Multiple papers).  
   - Use when elevating institutional roles; avoid for definitions without public function.  
6. **Threshold relocation (strong tendency)**  
   - Template: “The decisive threshold is not \[\[Old Threshold\]\] but \[\[New Threshold\]\].”  
   - Example: “The decisive test is not party autonomy but systemic legitimacy” (ADR scholarship).  
   - Use when altering decision rule; avoid if unchanged.  

### Derivation patterns
7. **Conditional obligation**  
   - Template: “If \[\[Condition\]\], then \[\[Institution\]\] must \[\[Action\]\].”  
   - Example: “If courts are to satisfy Article 6, they must ensure representation in complex cases.”  
   - Function: derive design rule; avoid when condition unproven.  
8. **Causal chain seal**  
   - Template: “\[\[Cause\]\] has led to \[\[Effect 1\]\], which in turn necessitates \[\[Effect 2\]\].”  
   - Example: “Austerity-driven staff cuts led to delays, which in turn necessitate procedural redesign.”  
   - Use to justify reform steps; avoid when causality weak.  
9. **Enumerated inference**  
   - Template: “This yields three consequences: \[\[Consequence1\]\]; \[\[Consequence2\]\]; \[\[Consequence3\]\].”  
   - Example: Triad closures in funding chapter.  
   - Use to summarize; avoid if fewer than two solid consequences.  
10. **Precondition formulation**  
    - Template: “A \[\[Prerequisite\]\] is a precondition for \[\[Outcome\]\].”  
    - Example: “Clear regulation is a precondition for sustainable litigation funding.”  
    - Use when gating future reforms; avoid for stylistic filler.  

### Concession patterns
11. **It might be said… but…**  
    - Template: “It might however be said that \[\[Objection\]\]. But \[\[Equivalence/Neutralisation\]\]; \[\[Regulatory Answer\]\].”  
    - Example: Contingency fees objection neutralised by regulation (Funding chapter).  
    - Use after stating preferred view; avoid as opener.  
12. **Limited acceptance**  
    - Template: “That point is valid in \[\[Narrow Context\]\]; it does not extend to \[\[Broader Context\]\].”  
    - Example: Acceptance of lawyerless efficiencies in simple claims only.  
    - Use to show fairness; avoid if broader context actually affected.  
13. **Modal hedge**  
    - Template: “It may/might \[\[Be/Follow\]\] that \[\[Claim\]\]; \[\[Constraint\]\] nonetheless remains.”  
    - Example: “Technology may reduce necessity in many cases; the constitutional function remains.”  
    - Use for empirical uncertainty; avoid for principles.  

### Elevation patterns
14. **Constitutional elevation**  
    - Template: “\[\[Issue\]\] is ultimately about \[\[Constitutional Principle\]\], not \[\[Operational Detail\]\].”  
    - Example: “Online courts are ultimately about open justice, not interface design.”  
    - Use when pivoting to principle; avoid overuse.  
15. **Closing crystallisation**  
    - Template: “Whatever the next step in \[\[Domain\]\] may be, it will and must be consistent with \[\[Principle\]\].”  
    - Example: Woolf reforms closing sentence.  
    - Use for final sentence of section/article; avoid mid-body.  
16. **Historical trajectory**  
    - Template: “From \[\[Past Reform\]\] through \[\[Intermediate Reform\]\] to \[\[Present Debate\]\], the same \[\[Driver\]\] persists.”  
    - Example: Champerty to modern funding liberalisation.  
    - Use to show continuity; avoid if driver differs.

## Component 4 — The Hedging and Assertion Map  
*Purpose: prescribe exact modality per claim type with a decision rule.*

| Claim type | Assertion/hedge ladder (strongest → strongest hedge) |
| --- | --- |
| Doctrinal claim about existing law | “is” / “requires” / “established that” → “appears to require” → “may require” |
| Normative claim about what law should be | “must” / “ought to” / “essential that” → “should” → “may need to” |
| Empirical claim about operation in practice | “evidence shows” / “data demonstrate” → “studies suggest” → “it is not clear that” / “it may be that” |
| Historical claim about reform trajectory | “has consistently” / “has repeatedly” → “has often” → “at times” / “occasionally” |
| Constitutional/systemic obligation claim | “is a constitutional requirement” / “paramount” / “non-negotiable” → “fundamentally important” → “important consideration” |

Decision rule: if uncertain which modality applies, default to the **middle hedge** for that claim type (“appears to / should / studies suggest / has often / fundamentally important”), then escalate to stronger assertion only when two or more named authorities or data points are cited; downgrade to strongest hedge when evidence is incomplete.

## Component 6 — The Six Signature Moves — Execution Guide  
*Purpose: operational guides for the six moves; confidence noted where below confirmation threshold.*

1. **Constitutional elevation move (confirmed signature)**  
   - Function: shift debate to constitutional metric.  
   - Trigger: after presenting counter-argument or empirical efficiency claim.  
   - Steps: (a) Name operational debate; (b) State constitutional principle; (c) Reclassify issue as serving that principle; (d) Assert obligation (“must”).  
   - Example: “Technology may reduce necessity in many cases; the constitutional function of open justice remains.” (Justice Without Lawyers)  
   - Template: “\[\[Operational Issue\]\] is ultimately about \[\[Constitutional Principle\]\]; therefore \[\[Institution\]\] must \[\[Action\]\].”  
2. **Historical trajectory move (strong tendency; 5/10)**  
   - Function: show continuity of driver.  
   - Trigger: introducing reform proposals.  
   - Steps: (a) Identify past reform; (b) Identify intermediate reform; (c) Name persistent driver; (d) Draw present implication.  
   - Example: Champerty → CFAs → third-party funding share same access rationale.  
   - Template: “From \[\[Past\]\] through \[\[Middle\]\] to \[\[Present\]\], the persistent driver has been \[\[Driver\]\]; any future reform must \[\[Design Response\]\].”  
3. **Reclassification move (confirmed signature)**  
   - Function: change evaluative frame.  
   - Trigger: when efficiency discourse dominates.  
   - Steps: (a) State common frame; (b) Deny sufficiency; (c) State new frame; (d) Derive new metric.  
   - Example: “The question is really one of design and superiority.” (Funding chapter)  
   - Template: “\[\[Target\]\] is not \[\[Common Frame\]\]; it is \[\[New Frame\]\], requiring \[\[Metric\]\].”  
4. **Reform-as-obligation derivation (confirmed signature)**  
   - Function: convert policy choice to duty.  
   - Trigger: immediately after constitutional elevation.  
   - Steps: (a) State principle; (b) If-then obligation; (c) Name design constraints; (d) Declare precondition.  
   - Example: “Any future steps will have to grapple… a fuller understanding is a precondition.” (Funding chapter)  
   - Template: “If \[\[System\]\] is to satisfy \[\[Principle\]\], reform must \[\[Constraint\]\]; \[\[Prerequisite\]\] is a precondition.”  
5. **Anticipatory concession (confirmed signature)**  
   - Function: address strongest objection before it is raised.  
   - Trigger: after asserting preferred view.  
   - Steps: (a) “It might however be said that \[\[Objection\]\]”; (b) Equivalence/neutralisation; (c) Regulatory/design fix; (d) Residual uncertainty (“not clear that…”).  
   - Example: Contingency fee objection neutralised by regulation.  
   - Template: “It might however be said that \[\[Objection\]\]. But the same could just as well be said of \[\[Parallel\]\]; \[\[Regulatory Fix\]\]; it is not clear that \[\[Residual Risk\]\].”  
6. **Closing crystallisation (confirmed signature)**  
   - Function: end with constraint + warning.  
   - Trigger: final sentence of section/article.  
   - Steps: (a) “Whatever the next step…”; (b) State governing principle; (c) Mandatory consistency; (d) Warn of cost.  
   - Example: Woolf reforms closing sentence.  
   - Template: “Whatever the next step in \[\[Domain\]\] may be, it will and must be consistent with \[\[Principle\]\]; otherwise \[\[Systemic Cost\]\] will follow.”

## Component 7 — The Citation Integration Protocol  
*Purpose: ensure two writers place citations identically.*

1. **Placement**: default to sentence-end footnotes; insert mid-sentence only when the authority is the grammatical subject (e.g., “Lord Woolf held that…”).  
2. **Quotation vs paraphrase**: paraphrase by default; quote only when wording is itself argumentative (definitions, constitutional formulations).  
3. **Cases/statutes as subjects**: use as subject when the holding is the operative rule (“Article 6 requires…”); otherwise place authority in footnote after proposition.  
4. **Academic authority introduction**: name the author in text when contrasting views or using signature theory; otherwise cite in footnote only.  
5. **String citations**: limit to three; order by authority (statute → appellate case → article); separate with semicolons in footnote.  
6. **Qualified authorities**: if authority is partial/outdated, flag in text with hedge (“\[\[Author\]\] suggests… although based on pre-reform data”) before footnote.  
7. **Embedded questions**: when closing with a forward question, place citation before the question mark.  
8. **Rule for mixed sentences**: when a sentence contains both a paraphrase and your inference, place the citation immediately after the paraphrase clause, then continue with inference; do not place at the end.  
9. **No naked assertions**: every doctrinal or empirical claim requires a footnote unless already introduced as undisputed (“beyond question”).

## Component 8 — The Golden Thread Discipline  
*Purpose: maintain a single analytical concept across the essay with escalating force.*

1. **Introduction**: name the thread once, plainly, without over-defining (“The golden thread is \[\[Concept\]\]: the guarantee that \[\[Short Value\]\].”).  
2. **Section openings**: begin each section with one sentence linking back to the thread (“In \[\[Section Topic\]\], the golden thread requires \[\[Specific Duty\]\].”).  
3. **Section closings**: end with a constraint sentence invoking the thread (“This section shows that without \[\[Thread Element\]\], \[\[Cost\]\] follows.”).  
4. **Escalation**: each subsequent section must strengthen the thread by adding (a) broader scope, (b) stronger modality, or (c) tighter design rule.  
5. **Conclusion**: single crystallising sentence: “Because \[\[Concept\]\] secures \[\[Value\]\], any future step must \[\[Design Constraint\]\].”

Worked example (concept: **open justice as participation guarantee**) across five sections:  
- Section 1 opening: “In defining the problem, the golden thread requires public participation as the baseline.”  
- Section 1 closing: “Without participation, efficiency claims lack legitimacy.”  
- Section 2 opening: “In examining empirical pressures, the thread demands that digital reforms preserve visibility.”  
- Section 2 closing: “Visibility must remain even where automation improves speed.”  
- Section 3 opening: “When testing counter-arguments, the thread requires proof that participation is unharmed.”  
- Section 3 closing: “Absent that proof, lawyerless models are confined to simple claims.”  
- Section 4 opening: “In deriving reform, the thread mandates design features that enable lay monitoring.”  
- Section 4 closing: “Design without monitoring breaches the thread.”  
- Conclusion: “Because open justice secures participation, any online process must be designed for public visibility and review.”

## Component 9 — The One-Page Pre-Submission Checklist  
*Purpose: 3-minute, yes/no enforcement per paragraph.*

### Section A — Architecture (5 items)
1. Does the paragraph use an approved opening (timeline/definition/necessity/binary question)?  
2. Is the claim-evidence-application ratio within Sorabji bounds (claim ≥50%, evidence 20–35%, application 10–20%)?  
3. Is the paragraph’s closing move one of the approved closures (question hook/triad/consequence/constraint)?  
4. Is the counter-argument, if present, placed before the pivot to constitutional necessity?  
5. Are concessions embedded rather than quarantined in a separate paragraph?  

### Section B — Prose Quality (7 items)
6. Does every doctrinal/empirical assertion carry a citation?  
7. Is hedging language matched to claim type per the map?  
8. Are transitions explicit (“however/therefore/in other words” present as needed)?  
9. Are sentences long only when qualified and still spine-visible (one main clause + modifiers)?  
10. Are triads correctly enumerated and limited to three items?  
11. Are incompatible register words absent and structural vocabulary present where moves occur?  
12. Is the closing sentence free of colloquial emphasis and contains a constraint if it ends a section?  

### Section C — Analytical Integrity (5 items)
13. Does the paragraph advance the golden thread explicitly or implicitly?  
14. Does it either develop the positive case or present the strongest counter-case (not both muddled)?  
15. If using a signature move, are all steps executed in order?  
16. Are reform proposals framed as obligations tied to a named principle?  
17. If a question is posed, is it answered or directed to the next paragraph with a clear hook?  

If any answer is “No,” revise before submission.
